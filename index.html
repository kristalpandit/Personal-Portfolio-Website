About

I'm a student at Valmiki Shiksha Sadan in Bharatpur, Nepal, where I served as School Captain in Grade 9 and later founded the school's Mathematics Club. Outside the classroom, I've led a team through a 20-site groundwater quality study in my hometown, and co-founded a small foundation that raises funds for underprivileged children in Chitwan and Kathmandu.

I care most about the work that happens when nobody's grading it — starting a club that didn't exist, running a field study with real lab methods, or checking in on kids at an orphanage between exams. As part of the Incubate Nepal 2026 cohort, I worked on GPT Quantifiers, a research project measuring how much published scientific writing shows signs of AI authorship — and I'm always looking for the next thing worth building.

Research

Incubate Nepal 2026 Cohort · Natural Language Processing

GPT Quantifiers

Estimating how much of scientific writing is LLM-modified — replicating Liang et al.'s framework on a developing-country corpus.

5 fields covered
2022–24 monthly window
28.2% peak est. α, CS, Mar 2024
±0.9–1.6 pt validation deviation

Existing estimates of LLM-modified scientific writing come from arXiv and bioRxiv — research published outside those venues had never been measured. Our project implemented Liang et al.'s maximum-likelihood framework for estimating α, the fraction of LLM-modified sentences, on monthly abstracts pulled from Open Journal Systems across five fields: Medicine, Education, Business, History, and Computer Science.

We validated the estimator against synthetic mixtures of human and AI-rewritten text with known α, recovering the true fraction to within 0.9–1.6 percentage points at the low-to-mid range where our real data sits. At very high α (60–80%) the estimator reads conservatively, so our field-level numbers are a floor, not a ceiling.

Every field was flat until ChatGPT's release on 30 November 2022, then climbed. Computer Science separated from the pack first and never rejoined — by March 2024 it sat at 5.1× the estimated α of History, the lowest field.

We had AI rewrite our own writing as part of testing the pipeline — plots and characters came back wrong. No historian wants their reading distorted, which is part of why fields like History use these tools so much more warily.

Environmental Science · Project Leader

Groundwater Quality Analysis in Bharatpur

Led a team of 4 students, under the guidance of 2 teachers, testing groundwater quality across 20 sites in Bharatpur Metropolitan City.

20 sample sites
50% samples coliform-positive
65% above hardness threshold
20% at/above WHO arsenic limit

Groundwater is the main water source for many households in Bharatpur, often used without routine testing. We collected 20 samples across the metropolitan area over two days in mid-July 2025 and tested them for seven things that matter for safe drinking water: pH, arsenic, coliform bacteria, hardness (calcium and magnesium), chloride, iron, and nitrate. We used standard lab methods and field test kits for each one, and brought in a professional laboratory for the more advanced measurements.

The results were mixed rather than uniformly safe. Half of all samples tested positive for coliform — the most immediate public-health concern, since chemical readings can look fine while the water is still microbiologically unsafe. Hardness was the most widespread chemical pattern, with 13 of 20 samples above the 180 mg/L threshold. Arsenic wasn't common, but where it appeared it was severe: one sample reached 100 ppb against a WHO limit of 10. Iron and nitrate, by contrast, were within safe limits in every single sample.

We recommended repeat testing at the highest-risk sites, household treatment (boiling, disinfection, and filtration), re-testing coliform-positive sources to check whether contamination is persistent, and a larger, seasonal follow-up survey.
